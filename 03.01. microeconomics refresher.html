Scarcity, choice and opportunity cost: what an hour of staff time is worth

Part 1: scarcity and choice

Economics starts from one observation: scarcity. A resource is scarce when there is less of it than people would like to use. Money is scarce, but for Boréal Studio the scarce resource is more concrete: staff time. Five people, about eight working hours each per day, and every hour that goes to sorting e-mail is an hour that does not go to a client. A scarce resource forces a choice, and a choice has two sides: what you take, and what you leave.

Microeconomics is the part of economics that studies these choices one decision-maker at a time: a household, a firm, an owner. The owner of the studio asked one question in Class 1: which routine should we automate first, how much time would it really save, and is a free assistant good enough? Each word of that question is a microeconomic word. "First" asks for a ranking; "how much" asks for a measurement; "good enough" asks for a comparison between the free option and a paid one. This hour gives you the four tools that answer it: the cost of a routine, marginal analysis, the cost categories, and break-even with payback.

One remark before the first tool. In Class 2 you wrote RQ (research question) 2: what is an hour of staff time worth, what does automation cost, and where is the break-even between the free tier and a paid assistant? Today's four lessons are the method of that research question, and Brick 3 is its first answer. Nothing here is theory for its own sake; every formula becomes a column in your script this afternoon and a method of the EconEngine class in Phase 2.

Slides 7–8

Part 2: the cost of a routine

The first tool converts minutes into dollars. A routine run takes a number of minutes; a month contains a number of runs; and an hour of the person who runs it has a value. The cost of a routine for one month is therefore:

cost per month = minutes per run ÷ 60 × hourly value × runs per month

Take e-mail triage. In the studio's file it takes about 35 minutes a run when done by hand. Each of the five staff members runs it once a day, and a month has 20 working days, so the studio runs it 100 times a month. At the given hourly value of 28 $ per hour:

35 ÷ 60 × 28 × 100 = 1 633.33 $ per month

That is the number you will compute on the board in Activity 1, and it is the number Code example 3.1.A reads from the file. The same formula, applied to the six routines, gives the studio's monthly routine bill: about 5 800 $ a month and about 208 staff hours, of which e-mail triage is the largest line (28 %).

Where does the hourly value come from? The owner gave 28 $ per hour, and the word that matters is loaded: the loaded cost of an hour is the salary for that hour plus the employer's overhead for it (employer contributions, vacation pay, equipment, the office). It is what the hour costs the studio, not what the employee sees on a pay slip. A real figure starts from published wage data, for example the earnings tables of Statistics Canada, and adds the overhead of the business. For the project, 28 $ per hour is a given number: use it everywhere, and write it once at the top of your script as a named constant, never as a number repeated in formulas.

Slide 9

Part 3: opportunity cost

The second idea is the one this lesson is named after. The 1 633 $ above is an explicit cost: money the studio actually pays, hour by hour, for the time spent on e-mail. But a choice also has a hidden side. The opportunity cost of using an hour in one way is the value of the next-best use of that same hour, the best alternative that was given up. If a consultant's hour could have been sold to a client at 65 $, then an hour spent sorting e-mail by hand gives up 65 $ of revenue, whatever the salary for that hour was.

Opportunity cost is why "free" software still costs something. A free assistant has no price, but learning it, setting it up and checking its output take hours, and those hours have a next-best use. When Brick 3 values the team's set-up hours at the same 28 $ per hour, it is applying exactly this idea: the hours are not free because nobody paid for them; they are costly because they could have been used otherwise.

Two consequences for your script. First, the hourly value is an input, not a fact of nature: the same minutes give a different bill at 28 $ and at 65 $, so your program must take it as a parameter and your notes must say which value you used and why. Second, opportunity cost is always relative to an alternative. "What else could this hour have done?" is the question that turns a time log into a business decision, and Code example 3.1.A ends by asking it: the 208 routine hours of the studio are worth about 5 800 $ at the loaded cost, and about 13 500 $ of client work at the billable rate. Both numbers are correct; they answer different questions.

      Slides 17–18
      Part 1: thinking at the margin
      The owner will not automate everything at once. She will automate one routine, then decide about the next one, then the next. Economists call a decision about one more unit a decision at the margin, and the method that compares the benefit and the cost of that one more unit is marginal analysis. The unit here is one routine (or, later in this lesson, one more level of automation of the same routine).
      

      The marginal benefit, written MB (marginal benefit), of automating a routine is the value of the time it saves, per month. The saving per run is the difference between the minutes of a manual run and the minutes of an assisted run, minus the minutes a person still spends reviewing the assistant's work; the value follows the formula of Lesson 3.1:
      

      

      MB per month = minutes saved per run ÷ 60 × hourly value × runs per month
      

      

      The marginal cost, written MC (marginal cost), of automating the routine is what that one more automation costs, per month. It has two parts. The first is the cost of the requests the assistant sends to the language-model API (Application Programming Interface): zero inside the free quota, and the price per request beyond it. The second is a share of the set-up: the hours the team spends building and testing the tool, valued at the hourly value, spread over the planning horizon (twelve months in this course) to obtain a monthly amount:
      

      

      MC per month = paid requests × price per request + set-up cost ÷ horizon in months
      

      

      Both numbers are in dollars per month, so they can be compared. That comparison is the rule of this lesson.
      Slides 19–20
      Part 2: the rule and the ranking
      The decision rule of marginal analysis is one line: automate while MB ≥ MC. If the extra benefit of automating a routine is at least as large as its extra cost, automating it makes the studio better off; if not, it does not, whatever the routine's total cost in Lesson 3.1 was. The difference MB − MC is the net benefit of the automation, and ranking the routines by net benefit, largest first, answers the owner's word "first".
      

      Apply it to e-mail triage. The pilot rows of the file show that the three participants took 35.9 minutes by hand and 13.7 minutes with the assistant: 22.1 minutes saved per run, 20.1 once two minutes of review are subtracted. One hundred runs a month at 28 $ per hour:
      

      

      MB = 20.1 ÷ 60 × 28 × 100 = 938 $ per month
      

      

      The tool sends three requests per run, 300 a month, far inside a free quota of 3 000 (a teaching assumption: the real number is the one you read on the provider's page and post with its date). The set-up is estimated at 40 hours, 1 120 $, or 93.33 $ a month over twelve months:
      

      

      MC = 0 + 1 120 ÷ 12 = 93.33 $ per month, so MB − MC = 844.67 $ per month: automate.
      

      

      Code example 3.2.A does the same for the six routines. Five pass the rule; the invoice check does not: the assistant saves five minutes a run, but a person must verify every flagged invoice for six minutes, so the net saving is negative and the routine stays manual, for now. The ranking by net benefit puts e-mail triage first (845 $), then social posts (292 $), the daily report (233 $), scheduling (175 $) and the stand-up notes (35 $). That order is the first draft of the owner's automation plan, and of the recommendation you will defend in January.
      Slide 21
      Part 3: diminishing returns
      Why does the ranking matter, if everything that passes the rule is worth doing? Because of diminishing returns: as more of something is done, each additional unit brings less than the one before. The first tool the team builds saves the most (e-mail triage, 845 $ net), the second saves less, and the fifth adds 35 $ a month: in the cumulative column of Code example 3.2.A, each line adds less than the previous one. With a team of three students and fourteen weeks, the fourth tool costs the same effort as the first and returns far less; this is why the January test of Class 2 says that three tools that work beat ten that do not.
      

      Diminishing returns also operate inside one routine. Automating e-mail triage can be pushed in levels: sort and label the mailbox (15 minutes saved a run), then draft the replies (5 more minutes), then send them without review (2 more minutes). Each level is judged on its own extra benefit and extra cost, not on the totals so far. In the example the first two levels pass (700 $ against 47 $, then 233 $ against 58 $) and the third does not (93 $ against 105 $ of extra set-up); the third level also carries a risk the numbers do not show: an e-mail sent by mistake. Stop at the first level where the extra MB no longer covers the extra MC. This is the same rule as before, applied one level at a time, and it is how the marginal_benefit() method of EconEngine will be used in Phase 2.

          Slide 28
          Part 1: fixed and variable costs
          Lesson 3.2 used two kinds of cost without naming them. Now we name them, because the names decide how a cost enters a formula. A fixed cost, written FC (fixed cost), is a cost that does not change with the quantity produced during the period: whether the assistant handles ten requests or ten thousand this month, the cost is the same. The paid assistant tier is a fixed cost per period: 20 $ per user per month, 100 $ a month for the five seats, whatever the usage. The team's set-up time is a fixed cost too, of a different kind: it is paid once, at the start, not every month; 40 hours at 28 $ per hour is 1 120 $.
          

          A variable cost, written VC (variable cost), changes with the quantity. Requests to the API (Application Programming Interface) beyond the free quota are a variable cost: each one adds the price per request. The staff minutes spent on a routine are a variable cost of running the routine: every run adds its minutes, and the whole bill of Lesson 3.1 was a variable cost. Inside the free quota, the variable cost of a request is zero up to the quota, then rises at the price per request: the cost curve has a kink at the quota, and Figure 3.3 shows it.
          

          The total cost, TC (total cost), of the period is the sum: TC = FC + VC. It is the number to compare between two options over the same period and the same quantity, and the number to recompute when an assumption changes. The classification of a cost is not a property of the money but of the question: a subscription is fixed with respect to the number of requests but variable with respect to the number of users (one more user, 20 $ more). Always say "fixed with respect to what".
          Slides 29–30
          Part 2: average and marginal cost
          Two more numbers come from the total cost. The average cost, AC (average cost), is the total cost divided by the quantity: AC = TC ÷ runs, the cost per routine run. The paid tier's average cost per run falls as the volume grows, because the same 100 $ is spread over more runs; the free tier's average cost is zero up to the quota, then rises slowly. The marginal cost, MC (marginal cost), is the change in total cost when the quantity changes by one unit: MC = ΔTC ÷ Δruns, or per request when the unit is a request. Inside the quota the marginal cost of one more request is 0 $; beyond it, it is the price per request, 0.02 $ in the teaching example. This is the number Lesson 3.2 used in the marginal cost of a routine, and the number that makes the free tier so attractive for a small studio: at 300 requests a month, every request is inside the quota.
          

          Code example 3.3.A tabulates all of this for volumes from zero to 12 000 requests a month: the variable, total, average and marginal cost of the free tier, and the total and average cost of the paid tier. The two total costs cross at 8 000 requests a month (quota plus 100 $ divided by 0.02 $). Below that volume the free tier costs less; above it the subscription does. The studio needs 300: the free tier wins by a wide margin, as long as the quota stays where it is, which is why Brick 3 asks you to record the quota with the date you read it. A quota is a number that can change next month; your model must say where it enters.
          

          A warning about the units. Average cost is "per run" or "per request"; marginal cost is "per additional run" or "per additional request"; fixed cost is "per period" or "once". Mixing them is the second most common error in this topic, after the one of Part 3. Write the unit next to every number in your script's output, as the code examples do.
          Slide 31
          Part 3: the sunk cost
          The team spent 40 hours building the e-mail tool. Those hours are gone: they cannot be recovered whether the pilot continues or stops. A cost that has already been incurred and that no present or future decision can recover is a sunk cost. The rule is short and absolute: a sunk cost must not influence a decision, because it is the same under every alternative. A decision compares what changes from here on; a sunk cost changes nothing.
          

          The trap looks like this. In month one of the pilot, the benefit is 938 $ and the running cost is 28 $ (one hour of maintenance). Someone subtracts the 1 120 $ of set-up and announces a loss of 210 $: "the pilot is losing money, stop it." The computation is wrong, not arithmetically but logically. Stopping the pilot does not return the 1 120 $; it only forfeits the 910 $ of net saving every month. Looking forward only, the choice is between +910 $ a month (continue) and 0 $ (stop), and the set-up appears in neither column.
          

          Where, then, does the set-up cost belong? In two places only. Before the decision to build, it is a future cost, and it enters the marginal cost of Lesson 3.2 as a share per month. After the decision, it enters the payback computation of Lesson 3.4 as the amount to recover: a question of how long, not of whether to continue. Code example 3.3.A ends by printing the right and the wrong comparison side by side; in Phase 2, EconEngine.payback_months() will take the set-up cost as a parameter, and no method will ever deduct it from a future saving.

              Part 1: break-even hours
              The owner's question "is free good enough, or should I pay?" is a break-even question. The break-even point of an option is the quantity at which its benefit exactly covers its cost: below it the option loses, above it the option gains. For a paid assistant priced per month, the natural quantity is hours saved per month, and the break-even hours are:
              

              

              break-even hours = monthly cost of the paid option ÷ hourly value
              

              

              At 20 $ per user per month and 28 $ per hour: 20 ÷ 28 = 0.714 hours, about 43 minutes a month per user. If the paid assistant saves a user more than 43 minutes a month, the seat pays for itself; if it saves less, it does not. For the five seats together: 100 ÷ 28 = 3.57 hours a month. The pilot measured 20.1 net minutes saved per run of e-mail triage, 20 runs a month, 6.7 hours per user: nine times the break-even. The answer to the owner is not "free is good enough" or "pay": it is "any assistant that does what the pilot did pays for itself many times over; choose on fit and risk, not on the seat price".
              

              Note what the formula does not contain: the set-up hours. The break-even of a subscription compares a monthly price with a monthly saving; the one-time set-up belongs to the next number. And note the guard your engine will need: a division by an hourly value of zero or less makes no sense, so break_even_hours() raises a ValueError with a message instead of returning a meaningless number.

              Part 2: payback and make-or-buy
              The payback period answers the second question about the set-up: how long do the savings take to recover it? With a one-time fixed cost and a net saving each month:
              

              

              payback months = set-up cost ÷ monthly net saving
              

              

              Set-up 1 120 $; monthly net saving 939 − 28 = 911 $ (the benefit of 20.1 net minutes over 100 runs, minus one maintenance hour): 1.23 months. After five weeks the set-up hours are recovered, and every later month is a gain. Payback has a documented special case: when the monthly net saving is zero or negative, the set-up is never recovered, and the engine returns math.inf ("never") rather than crashing. Payback is a simple, widely used measure; it ignores what happens after the recovery and it does not discount the future, which is why your report will present it next to the monthly net saving, not alone.
              

              Make-or-buy is the comparison the owner will actually read: build the tool (JARVIS: a one-time set-up, then maintenance every month, requests inside the free quota) or buy a seat on a paid assistant (no set-up, a subscription every month). Over the same horizon, the total cost of each option is:
              

              

              build = set-up + maintenance per month × months  ·  buy = subscription per month × months
              

              

              Over 12 months: build 1 120 + 28 × 12 = 1 456 $; buy 100 × 12 = 1 200 $: buying costs less. Over 24 months: build 1 792 $; buy 2 400 $: building costs less. The horizon decides, because the set-up is paid once and the subscription is paid every month; and two things the numbers do not show decide too: whether the paid assistant does the task as well as the pilot did, and whether the free quota survives. Your decision candidate must say both.

                  What to remember
                  1. An hour of staff time has a value even when nobody pays for it: the loaded cost (28 $/h) is what the hour costs; the opportunity cost is the value of its next-best use. Cost of a routine = minutes ÷ 60 × hourly value × runs per month.
                  2. Decisions are taken at the margin: automate a routine (or add a level) while MB ≥ MC; rank by MB − MC; each added routine returns less than the previous one.
                  3. Name the cost category with respect to a stated quantity: fixed (subscription per period; set-up, paid once), variable (paid requests; staff minutes per run), total = FC + VC, average = TC ÷ runs, marginal = the change for one more unit, zero inside the free quota.
                  4. A sunk cost is spent under every alternative and must not influence a decision; the set-up enters MC before the decision and the payback after it, never as a deduction from future savings.
                  5. Break-even hours = monthly price ÷ hourly value (43 min a month per seat); payback months = set-up ÷ monthly net saving (1.23 months); make-or-buy depends on the horizon, on fit and on the quota risk.
                  6. Every formula maps to a column (minutes, tool, routine, staff_id, date), to a given number or to a stated assumption, and to one EconEngine method; the Formula Card starts today.
                  

                  Terms of the lesson
                  Scarcity
                  the situation of a resource of which there is less than people would like to use; it forces a choice.
                  Microeconomics
                  the study of the choices of individual decision-makers (a household, a firm, an owner) and of how prices and costs guide them.
                  Cost of a routine
                  minutes per run ÷ 60 × hourly value × runs per month, in dollars per month.
                  Loaded hourly cost
                  salary for the hour plus the employer's overhead for it; the given value is 28 $/h.
                  Explicit cost
                  a cost paid in money (the loaded cost of the hours spent).
                  Opportunity cost
                  the value of the next-best use of a resource, given up by the choice made.
                  Next-best use
                  the best alternative to the choice actually made; for a consultant's hour, billable client work.
                  Marginal analysis
                  the comparison of the extra benefit and the extra cost of one more unit of an activity.
                  Marginal benefit (MB)
                  the value of the time saved by one more automation: minutes saved ÷ 60 × hourly value × runs per month.
                  Marginal cost (MC)
                  the extra cost of that automation: paid requests × price per request + set-up cost ÷ horizon.
                  The MB ≥ MC rule
                  undertake the unit while its marginal benefit is at least its marginal cost; stop at the first unit that fails.
                  Net benefit
                  MB − MC; the ranking key of the candidates.
                  Diminishing returns
                  each additional unit of an activity brings less than the previous one.
                  Stated assumption
                  a number the analysis needs but has not measured, written in the open where it can be changed and discussed.
                  Fixed cost (FC)
                  a cost that does not change with the quantity during the period (the subscription per period; the set-up, paid once).
                  Variable cost (VC)
                  a cost that changes with the quantity (paid requests beyond the quota; staff minutes per run).
                  Total cost (TC)
                  FC + VC for the period and the quantity considered.
                  Average cost (AC)
                  TC ÷ quantity; the cost per run or per request.
                  Marginal cost (MC)
                  the change in total cost for one more unit; 0 $ per request inside the free quota, the price per request beyond it.
                  Kink
                  the point of a cost curve where the slope changes; the free tier's total cost has one at the quota.
                  Sunk cost
                  a cost already incurred that no present or future decision can recover; it must not influence a decision.
                  Break-even point
                  the quantity at which the benefit of an option exactly covers its cost.
                  Break-even hours
                  monthly cost of the paid option ÷ hourly value; 20 ÷ 28 = 0.71 h (43 min) per user per month.
                  Payback period
                  set-up cost ÷ monthly net saving, in months; math.inf when nothing is saved.
                  Monthly net saving
                  the monthly benefit of a tool minus its monthly running cost (maintenance, paid requests).
                  Make-or-buy
                  the comparison of the total cost of building (set-up + maintenance × months) with buying (subscription × months) over the same horizon.
                  Horizon
                  the number of months over which two options are compared, or over which a set-up is spread.
                  Decision candidate
                  a 120-word recommendation with the finding, the evidence, the decision and the risks; the Brick 3 deliverable docs/econ_notes.md.
